<Part kind="answer">

Each hair on your body has a tiny muscle. When it squeezes, it pulls the hair upright and pushes up a little bump of skin.

You have hairs almost everywhere, even where they're too fine to notice. Each one grows from a little pocket in your skin called a *follicle*, and almost every follicle has its own tiny muscle, the *arrector pili* ("hair raiser" in Latin).

You can't move these muscles on purpose. When you get cold, your nervous system switches them on automatically. Strong feelings can flip the same switch: fear, excitement or a song that gives you chills. That's your body releasing **adrenaline**.

For furry animals, this is really useful. Fluffed-up fur traps a thick layer of warm air, like a puffy jacket. It also makes an animal look bigger: think of a scared cat with a bottlebrush tail. A porcupine raises its quills the same way, because quills are special, extra-stiff hairs.

We humans have lost most of our fur, so our goosebumps don't keep us very warm. They're a leftover from our hairy ancestors, which is why they come with such a funny name: our bumpy skin looks like a plucked goose's.

</Part>

<Mystery>

Scientists used to think human goosebumps were just a leftover. But in 2020, researchers studying mice found that the nerve and muscle that make goosebumps also wake up the stem cells that grow new hair. In mice kept in the cold for a long time, this helps grow a thicker coat. Does it do anything for people? Nobody knows yet.

</Mystery>

<Whoa big="5 million">

You have about 5 million hair follicles on your body, and almost every one has its own goosebump muscle. Eyebrows and eyelashes don't, which is why they never stand on end.
