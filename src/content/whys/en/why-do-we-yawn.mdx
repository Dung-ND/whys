In 1987, scientists gave people air with extra oxygen, or extra carbon dioxide, to breathe. They yawned just as often, so oxygen isn't the answer. In 2007, other scientists showed people videos of yawning. People holding a **cool pack** to their forehead caught far fewer yawns than people holding a warm one. (The bars show the idea, not exact numbers.)

<Part kind="answer">

Nobody knows for sure! One leading idea is that a yawn helps cool your brain and perk you up.

Here's what we do know. A yawn is a long, deep breath in with your jaw wide open, a big stretch of the muscles in your face and neck, and then a quick breath out. It lasts about 6 seconds. Almost every animal with a backbone yawns: dogs, cats, lions, birds, even fish. Babies yawn before they're even born.

One idea is that **yawning cools the brain**. The big gulp of air and the stretch pump fresh, cooler blood through your head. That would explain why people yawn more when the air is cooler than their body (but not freezing), and why a cool pack on the forehead stops yawns.

Another idea is that yawning helps the brain **switch gears**: waking up, winding down for sleep, or getting ready for action. That fits with athletes yawning before a race.

And yawns are **contagious**. Seeing, hearing, or even reading about a yawn makes many people yawn. Yawns spread more easily between friends and family than between strangers, and young children usually don't catch them until they're about 4 or 5. Some scientists think this is linked to how we connect with the people around us.

</Part>

<Mystery>

Is a yawn really a brain cooler, or a gear-switcher, or both? And why do yawns spread from person to person, and even from people to dogs? Scientists are still running experiments, and different studies don't always agree. This is a question that's still wide open.

</Mystery>

</Myth>

<TryIt title="the contagious yawn test" need={['A friend or family member', 'A timer', 'A notebook and pencil']}>

1. Sit with your friend and chat normally for 3 minutes. Count how many times they yawn.
2. For the next 3 minutes, give a big, real-looking yawn every 30 seconds. Count their yawns again.
3. Did they yawn more the second time?
4. Try it with someone you know less well. Real scientists found that yawns spread more between people who are close.

</TryIt>
