<Part kind="answer">

The Moon is so far away that driving along barely changes the direction you see it in, so it seems to keep pace with you while everything nearby rushes past.

When you move, things around you seem to slide past in the other direction. How fast they slide depends on how far away they are. A fence post right beside the road flashes past in a blink. A tree a little farther away glides by. Mountains on the horizon creep along slowly.

The Moon is about **384,000 kilometers** away. Compared with that, even a long car trip barely moves you at all, so the direction to the Moon hardly changes. It stays in the same spot in your window.

Your brain is used to faraway things moving slowly and near things moving fast. When the trees and houses rush backward but the Moon doesn't, your brain gets the feeling that the Moon must be moving forward with you, following you.

Scientists call this effect **parallax**: things at different distances seem to shift by different amounts when you move. Astronomers use it to measure how far away the nearest stars are.

</Part>

<Myth claim="The Moon is following me.">

Everyone in every car sees the Moon "following" them, even cars driving in opposite directions! It isn't following anyone; it's just so far away that nobody's movement changes its direction.

</Myth>

<TryIt title="the finger test" need={['Your thumb', 'Something far away, like a tree or a building']}>

1. Hold your thumb up at arm's length, in front of something far away.
2. Close your left eye and look with your right. Then switch eyes.
3. Your thumb jumps sideways, but the faraway thing barely moves!
4. Now bring your thumb closer to your face and try again. The closer the thumb, the bigger the jump. That's parallax.

</TryIt>
