<Part kind="answer">

The Moon is always half lit by the Sun. As it travels around Earth, we see different amounts of its bright half.

The Moon makes no light of its own. It shines because sunlight bounces off it, like a ball in a flashlight beam. At every moment, the half facing the Sun is bright and the other half is dark.

The Moon goes all the way around Earth in about a month. (That's where the word *month* comes from!) Along the way:

- **New moon:** the Moon is between Earth and the Sun. Its bright side faces away from us, so we can't see it.
- **Waxing (growing):** night after night, more of the bright side turns toward us: a crescent, then a half moon, then a fat *gibbous* moon.
- **Full moon:** Earth is between the Sun and the Moon, and we see the whole bright side.
- **Waning (shrinking):** the same shapes in reverse, back to new moon.

One full cycle takes about **29.5 days**.

**Why is a half moon called a "quarter"?** Because the Moon has gone a quarter of the way around its trip.

**Upside down!** People in Australia or Argentina see the Moon's shapes flipped: a growing Moon is lit on the left instead of the right.

</Part>

<Myth claim="The phases are caused by Earth's shadow.">

Earth's shadow only reaches the Moon during a lunar eclipse, and that can only happen at full moon. The phases are about how much of the Moon's sunlit half we can see from here.

</Myth>

<TryIt title="the Moon in your hand" need={['A lamp without a lampshade', 'A ball, or an orange on a pencil', 'A dark room']} safety="Don't stare into the lamp, and keep it away from paper and cloth.">

1. Put the lamp on one side of the room. That's the Sun. Your head is Earth.
2. Hold the ball at arm's length, a little above your head.
3. Turn slowly in a circle, keeping your eyes on the ball.
4. Watch the lit part of the ball: crescent, half, full and back again. You just made every phase of the Moon!

</TryIt>
