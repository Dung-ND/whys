<Part kind="answer">

Rain slowly dissolves salts out of rocks, rivers carry them to the sea, and when sea water evaporates, the salt stays behind.

Rain picks up a little carbon dioxide from the air, which makes it very slightly acidic. As it soaks through soil and rock, it slowly dissolves tiny bits of minerals, including the ingredients of salt. Rivers carry these to the sea. River water holds so little salt that it tastes fresh.

In the sea, the Sun heats the surface and water evaporates: it turns into invisible vapor, which later becomes clouds and rain. But salt can't evaporate, so it stays in the sea. Over hundreds of millions of years, the sea has become salty.

Rivers aren't the only source. Much of the chloride (half of table salt) comes from gases bubbling out of volcanoes and hot vents on the seafloor.

Today, about **3.5%** of sea water is salt: roughly 35 grams in every liter, or about 6 teaspoons. Salt also leaves the sea slowly, buried in mud on the seafloor, so the saltiness has stayed about the same for a very long time.

</Part>

<Myth claim="If you're really thirsty, you can drink sea water.">

Sea water is saltier than your kidneys can handle. To get rid of all that salt, your body would lose more water than you drank, so you'd end up even thirstier. Sailors and desert cities make fresh water by taking the salt out first.

</Myth>

<TryIt title="make your own sea salt" need={['Half a cup of warm water', '1 teaspoon of salt', 'A dark plate', 'A sunny windowsill', 'A magnifying glass, if you have one']}>

1. Stir the salt into the warm water until it disappears.
2. Pour a thin layer onto the plate.
3. Leave it on a sunny windowsill for a few days.
4. The water vanishes into the air, but the salt stays. Look closely: the crystals are tiny cubes!

</TryIt>
